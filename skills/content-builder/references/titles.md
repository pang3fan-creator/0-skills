# Titles: H1, meta title, meta description

Three different jobs. Treating them as three copies of one sentence wastes the two most valuable surfaces on the page.

| Element | Job | Written for |
|---|---|---|
| **H1** | Says what this page is | The reader already on the page — and often the site's own navigation, cards and menus, which frequently reuse the same field |
| **Meta title** | The pitch in a list of competing results | Someone deciding whether to click |
| **Meta description** | The reason to click, in one breath | Someone skimming results, and increasingly an AI summarizing your page |

## The trap worth knowing

On many sites the H1 field is **not only** the on-page heading: navigation menus and content cards read the same value. A long "keyword-rich" H1 makes every menu item long too. A common, workable convention is **short H1 + enriched meta title** — the H1 states the thing, the meta title adds the qualifier. Check the project profile; if the site already does this, follow it.

## Practical rules

- **Decide which element carries the keyword.** H1 and meta title both need to be about the keyword, but they should not be the same string. Vary the qualifier (`template`, `free`, `online`, `editable`), not the keyword.
- **Lengths come from the site, not from theory.** Measure the existing pages of the same type (including any brand suffix the template appends) and stay inside that range. The usual killers are a title that gets truncated with the brand appended, or a description cut mid-sentence.
- **The description is not a summary.** A summary wastes the slot. Give the reader the outcome and the qualifier that makes it safe to click: what they get, what it costs, what is required.
- **Match the reader's words.** Use the phrasing people actually search (`how to make a work schedule`), not internal vocabulary ("schedule authoring flow").
- **No unverifiable claims** in these three fields — no ratings, no user counts, no superlatives that cannot be checked. This is where they are most tempting and most damaging.
- **Front-load what distinguishes you.** The first few words are what survives truncation.

## Checks before shipping

- Does any other page on the site already use this exact title or claim this keyword?
- Is the keyword present in the H1 **and** in the meta title — using the exact phrase, not a synonym, at least once?
- Is the brand suffix, if any, included in the length check?
- Read the three together: do they describe the same page, from three usefully different angles?
